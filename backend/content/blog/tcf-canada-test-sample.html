One example question from each of the four sections, in the exact format and difficulty style of the real exam.

Quick answer: TCF Canada has four sections: Listening (39 multiple-choice questions, 35 min), Reading (39 multiple-choice questions, 60 min), Writing (3 tasks, 60 min shared), and Speaking (3 tasks, ~12 min with an examiner). Below is one representative example question per section, matching the real format, plus what examiners are actually looking for in each.

Note: the examples on this page are original practice questions written to match the official TCF Canada format and difficulty — not reproduced questions from an actual past exam. Official past papers aren't published by France Éducation International, which is exactly why realistic, format-accurate practice matters.

Sample listening question

Real listening questions get progressively harder — early questions test simple factual comprehension, later ones test inference and tone. Here's a mid-difficulty example:

You hear: "Attention, le train à destination de Montréal partira du quai numéro 4 au lieu du quai numéro 2. Nous nous excusons pour la gêne occasionnée."

Question: Qu'est-ce qui a changé ?

  • A) L'heure de départ du train
  • B) Le quai de départ du train
  • C) La destination du train

Correct answer: B — the announcement changes the platform (quai), not the time or destination. This tests whether you can isolate the one changed detail inside a longer announcement, which is exactly the skill later listening questions demand at higher difficulty.

Sample reading question

Reading questions draw from notices, short articles, and practical texts. Here's an example based on a workplace notice:

You read: "Avis à tout le personnel : la réunion mensuelle prévue jeudi est reportée à vendredi à la même heure, en raison d'un conflit d'horaire avec la direction. La salle de réunion reste inchangée."

Question: Qu'est-ce qui reste identique ?

  • A) Le jour de la réunion
  • B) L'heure et la salle
  • C) La direction

Correct answer: B — the day changes (Thursday to Friday), but the time and room stay the same. Reading questions like this reward careful attention to what specifically changed versus what didn't, under real time pressure — 60 minutes for 39 questions is roughly 92 seconds each.

Sample writing prompts (all 3 tâches)

Writing shares one 60-minute timer across three tasks with different word ranges and purposes:

Tâche 1 — short practical message (60–120 words)

Vous devez annuler un rendez-vous chez le médecin prévu la semaine prochaine. Écrivez un message au secrétariat pour expliquer la situation et demander un nouveau rendez-vous.

Tâche 2 — descriptive/narrative piece (120–150 words)

Décrivez un changement important dans votre vie au cours des cinq dernières années et expliquez comment il vous a transformé(e).

Tâche 3 — argumentative essay (120–180 words)

Certaines personnes pensent que le télétravail améliore la qualité de vie, d'autres pensent qu'il isole les employés. Donnez votre opinion et justifiez-la.

Examiners grade writing on task achievement, coherence and cohesion, lexical range, and morphosyntax (grammar and spelling) as separate criteria — not just "was it correct French." A grammatically perfect answer that doesn't actually address the prompt scores lower than a slightly imperfect one that does.

Sample speaking prompts (all 3 tâches)

Tâche 1 — guided interview (~2 min)

Présentez-vous : parlez de votre parcours, de vos habitudes ou de vos centres d'intérêt.

Tâche 2 — interactive roleplay (~5.5 min including prep)

Vous appelez une agence de location de voitures pour réserver un véhicule pour le week-end. Posez des questions formelles et informelles pour obtenir les informations dont vous avez besoin.

Tâche 3 — opinion monologue (~4.5 min)

Partir s'installer à l'étranger est plus simple quand on est jeune. Partagez-vous cette affirmation ? Développez un argument structuré.

Tâche 2 is where candidates most often prepare the wrong way — it's the only task where you ask the questions, not the examiner, and a memorized monologue scores badly there because it isn't actually demonstrating the interactive skill being tested.

Why practising under real format matters

TCF Canada rewards familiarity with its specific structure almost as much as raw French ability. A few numbers worth knowing before you start: reading gives you roughly 92 seconds per question across 39 questions in 60 minutes; listening plays each audio clip exactly once, with no replay; and writing splits 60 minutes across three tasks with no individually enforced timer per task, meaning pacing is entirely your own responsibility. Candidates who only study grammar and vocabulary in isolation, without ever feeling these specific pressures, consistently underperform their actual French level on exam day simply because the format itself is unfamiliar.

How to actually use sample questions

  • Time yourself from question one, not just on "real" attempts. The pacing pressure is part of what a sample question should teach you, not something to add later.
  • Don't just check if you got it right — check why the wrong options are wrong. Listening and reading distractors are built to be plausible; understanding the trap is more useful than just confirming the answer.
  • For writing and speaking, get every sample response corrected against real criteria. Self-checking grammar alone misses task achievement and coherence, which are graded just as heavily.
  • Use samples to find your weak section, then go deeper there specifically. One question per skill (like this page) tells you the format; you need full-length mocks to find out which section is actually your weak point.

Frequently asked questions

Are official TCF Canada past papers publicly available?

No — France Éducation International doesn't publish official past exam papers. This is exactly why format-accurate practice questions, built to match the real difficulty and structure, are the closest substitute available.

How similar are practice questions to the real exam?

Well-built practice questions match the real exam's format, timing, word ranges, and difficulty progression closely, even though the specific content differs from whatever appears on your actual test date. The skills being tested — pacing, distinguishing plausible distractors, hitting task criteria — transfer directly.

How many sample questions should I do before the real exam?

There's no fixed number, but a full mock across all four skills early in your preparation (to find your weakest section) followed by concentrated practice on that section specifically is more effective than doing a handful of questions spread evenly across skills you're already strong in.

Do sample questions get harder as you go, like the real exam?

The real listening and reading sections use a progressive-difficulty structure where later questions are worth more and are harder. Good practice material should mirror that same progression, not present every question at uniform difficulty.

What's the fastest way to see my actual level, not just try sample questions?

A full-length mock exam across all four skills with CLB-aligned scoring gives you an actual estimated level, rather than a sense of whether individual questions felt easy or hard.

Should I do sample questions in French or check them against an English translation?

Work through them entirely in French first — the real exam gives you no translation support, so building the habit of reasoning about distractors and prompts in French directly is part of what makes practice transfer to exam day.
