How each section works

Listening practice

Each mock reproduces the real exam's structure: 39 multiple-choice questions from short audio clips, increasing in difficulty, each clip played once only. You'll get an instant score out of 699 plus its CLB-equivalent level, so you know immediately where that attempt lands — not just whether your answers were right or wrong.

Reading practice

Same format logic: 39 multiple-choice questions from notices, articles, and short texts, in rising order of difficulty, under a real 60-minute timer. Because reading is as much a pacing test as a comprehension one, practicing under the actual clock — not reading at your own pace and checking answers after — is what actually transfers to exam day.

Writing evaluation

Submit a response to a real tâche prompt (across the three task types — short message, narrative/descriptive piece, and argumentative essay) and get back a full annotated correction: every error highlighted and explained by category (prepositions, spelling, conjugation, agreement, anglicisms, and C1-level style improvements), plus an estimated CLB level, suggested vocabulary, and useful linking phrases.

Speaking evaluation

Record a response to a real speaking tâche and receive a full transcript with inline corrections, an estimated CLB level, and specific suggestions — the same structure as the writing evaluation, adapted for spoken performance.

How to get the most out of your free practice test

Take one full mock across all four skills first, before drilling any single one — this is the only way to actually find your weakest skill rather than guess at it.

Treat the timer as real from the very first attempt. Practicing untimed and then "adding pressure later" builds the wrong instincts.

Read every correction's explanation, not just the fixed version — the reason behind a mistake is what stops you repeating it.

Retake the same tâche type after a correction, not just a new random one, so you can see whether the specific error actually got fixed.

Once you've found your weakest skill, weight your remaining free attempts toward it rather than spreading them evenly.

Common questions before you start

A few things worth knowing before your first attempt, since they shape how useful the practice actually ends up being:

Take it somewhere quiet, timed, without pausing. A mock done with the video paused and a dictionary open tells you very little about how you'll perform under real conditions.

Your first attempt is a baseline, not a verdict. Most candidates' first full mock underestimates their real level slightly, simply because the format itself is unfamiliar the first time through.

A low score on one skill isn't a reason to panic — it's the whole point. Finding the weak skill now, for free, is strictly better than finding it on the official result after paying for the real exam.
