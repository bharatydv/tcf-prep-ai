Everything you need to know about the exam that decides your French-language CLB level for Express Entry, citizenship, and provincial nomination.

If you're reading this, you probably already know you need the TCF Canada — the question is what's actually on it, how it's scored, and how to walk in ready. This page is the short version of everything on this site about the exam: what the four papers cover, how your results convert to a CLB level, where to sit the test, and how to prepare for each section without wasting time on the wrong one.

What is TCF Canada?

The Test de connaissance du français pour le Canada (TCF Canada) is a French-proficiency exam designed and administered by France Éducation international, specifically formatted for Canadian immigration purposes. It's one of two French tests IRCC accepts for Express Entry, citizenship applications, and several provincial nominee programs — the other being TEF Canada.

Unlike a general TCF test you might take for a French university application, TCF Canada is a distinct version: it always includes all four skills in one sitting, uses IRCC's specific score-to-CLB conversion table, and produces a result formatted the way immigration officers expect to see it.

Quick answer: TCF Canada tests Listening, Reading, Writing, and Speaking in a single sitting, takes just under 3 hours total, costs roughly $280–$320 CAD in Canada (₹25,000–₹30,000 in India), and your result is valid for 2 years.

Who actually needs TCF Canada?

Not every French test counts for Canadian immigration, and not every applicant needs one at all — but for a large and growing group of candidates, TCF Canada is either mandatory or the single highest-leverage thing they can do to their file. You most likely need it if you fall into one of these groups:

Express Entry candidates who want to claim French-language CRS points, including the significant bilingual bonus available when you're also strong in English.

Candidates targeting French-language category-based draws, which have run with noticeably lower CRS cutoffs than general draws in recent rounds.

Work permit and PGWP holders in Canada who are approaching an application deadline and need a recent, valid language result on file.

Citizenship applicants aged 18–54, for whom proof of language ability at CLB 4 is a mandatory requirement.

Provincial Nominee Program (PNP) applicants in provinces that set a minimum French CLB level for specific streams.

If you're unsure which of these applies to you, the practical test is simple: check the specific program page on IRCC's site for a listed minimum language requirement. If one exists, TCF Canada (or TEF Canada) is one of only two ways to prove it in French.

The four papers: format, timing, and question count

Every candidate sits all four sections on the same day. Two are multiple-choice and machine-scored; two are graded by trained human examiners.

Section Format Questions / Tasks Duration Scored out of
Listening (Compréhension orale) Multiple choice, audio played once 39 questions 35 minutes 699
Reading (Compréhension écrite) Multiple choice 39 questions 60 minutes 699
Writing (Expression écrite) 3 written tasks, one shared timer 3 tâches 60 minutes 20
Speaking (Expression orale) Face-to-face with an examiner 3 tâches ~12 minutes 20

Total seat time runs close to 2 hours 47 minutes once you add the two comprehension papers to writing and speaking — plan for a half-day commitment including check-in.

Listening (Compréhension orale)

39 multiple-choice questions from short audio clips — announcements, conversations, radio-style extracts — increasing in difficulty as you go. Each clip plays once only, and questions get progressively harder, so the back third of the section is where most candidates lose points. At 35 minutes for 39 questions, you're averaging roughly 54 seconds per question including the time the audio itself takes to play.

Reading (Compréhension écrite)

Also 39 multiple-choice questions, this time from written notices, articles, and short texts, again in rising order of difficulty. With 60 minutes for 39 questions, pacing is tighter than it feels at first — roughly 92 seconds per question — and it's easy to over-invest in an early easy question and run short on time for the harder ones at the end.

Writing (Expression écrite)

Three tâches sharing a single 60-minute timer, with no set time per task — pacing is entirely up to you:

Tâche 1: a short practical message (60–120 words) — an email, note, or message responding to a real-life situation.

Tâche 2: a longer descriptive or narrative piece (120–150 words) — recounting an experience or describing a situation in detail.

Tâche 3: an argumentative essay (120–180 words) — stating and defending an opinion on a given topic.

Writing past the accepted word range is one of the most common — and most avoidable — mistakes: examiners mark what fits inside the range, and the extra effort beyond it earns nothing.

Speaking (Expression orale)

Three tâches, roughly 12 minutes total, face-to-face with a live examiner:

Tâche 1: a short guided interview — introduce yourself and talk about your background, habits, or interests.

Tâche 2: an interactive roleplay — ask formal or informal questions to obtain specific information in a real-world scenario.

Tâche 3: an opinion monologue — deliver a structured argument defending your position on an abstract societal issue.

Tâche 2 is the one candidates most often over-prepare for the wrong way: it's the only task where you're the one asking the questions, and a rehearsed monologue scores badly there — the examiner is assessing your ability to react and adapt, not recite.

How your exam day actually runs

Knowing the shape of the day removes one whole layer of nerves. A typical TCF Canada sitting runs roughly like this:

Arrival and check-in: bring your passport (or the ID your center specifies) and your booking confirmation. Phones, smartwatches, and other electronics are not allowed in the exam room and must be switched off and stored.

Listening (35 minutes): the comprehension papers typically run first, back to back.

Reading (60 minutes): immediately follows listening, usually with a short break between the two.

Writing (60 minutes): either the same day after a longer break, or in some centers, the expression papers are scheduled separately from the comprehension papers — confirm your specific center's schedule when you book.

Speaking (~12 minutes): a one-on-one slot with an examiner, sometimes on a different time slot than the written papers depending on how your center schedules candidates through the day.

Because comprehension (listening + reading) and expression (writing + speaking) are marked completely differently — machine-scored multiple choice versus human-graded performance — some centers batch candidates through each pair separately rather than running all four back-to-back for every person. Always check your specific confirmation email for your actual schedule rather than assuming.

How TCF Canada is scored — and why there's no pass mark

There's no overall pass or fail. Each of the four skills produces its own independent score, which IRCC then converts to a CLB (Canadian Language Benchmark) level — sometimes labelled NCLC for French. Your result is a set of four levels, not one number, and whichever program you're applying under sets its own minimum requirement, usually per skill.

Why there's no single overall score

This trips up more candidates than almost anything else on the exam. Because each of the four skills is scored and converted independently, it's entirely possible to be strong in listening and reading while your writing or speaking sits a full level lower — and for most programs, it's that lowest score that determines what your file actually qualifies for. A candidate with Listening CLB 9, Reading CLB 8, Writing CLB 9, and Speaking CLB 6 doesn't have a "CLB 8 file" — for any requirement asking for a minimum across all four skills, they have a CLB 6 file. Plan your preparation time around your weakest skill, not your strongest one.

Four mistakes that cost points — and aren't about your French

These four issues show up across nearly every candidate's early practice attempts, and none of them are about language ability — they're about how the exam itself is built.

Treating all four sections as equally urgent. Your file is held down by whichever single skill is weakest — find that skill with a full mock exam before spending a month over-practicing one you're already strong in.

Writing past the accepted word range. Tâche 3 accepts 120–180 words; well past that, the examiner marks what fits inside the range and the extra effort earns nothing.

Preparing Speaking Tâche 2 as a rehearsed speech. It's the only task where you ask the questions — a prepared monologue scores badly there because it isn't testing what the task actually asks for.

Practicing without a clock running. The comprehension papers are as much a pacing test as a language test — 39 reading questions in 60 minutes is 92 seconds each, and that changes your strategy once you actually feel it.

Decide on your test location — see our India exam centers guide or Canada exam centers guide for a full list of confirmed cities and addresses.

Go to your chosen Alliance Française branch's own registration page directly, rather than a third-party site.

Check the next open session date. Popular centers publish sessions weeks or months ahead and can fill within days — sometimes within hours.

Have your passport (or the specific ID your center requires) scanned and ready before the booking window opens.

Register and pay online to confirm your seat — sessions are first-come, first-served and fees are non-refundable in almost all cases.

Save your convocation email. You'll need to bring the printout plus valid ID on exam day.

TCF Canada vs TEF Canada: do you have a choice?

Both TCF Canada and TEF Canada are accepted equally by IRCC and map to the identical CLB scale — neither is officially "easier," and neither gives you more CRS points than the other for the same result. The real decision usually comes down to which test is available at a center near you, and which format (TCF's more compact scoring range vs. TEF's wider point spread) you personally find more comfortable. Where to take TCF Canada

TCF Canada is administered exclusively through Alliance Française branches and a small number of affiliated centers — you cannot sit it at a random testing center the way you might with some other exams.

In India: currently confirmed at New Delhi, Bengaluru, Kolkata, and Bhopal — see our full India exam centers guide for addresses, contacts, and formats (computer- vs. paper-based).

In Canada: a national network of roughly 28+ centers including Ottawa, Toronto (4 GTA locations), Montréal, Vancouver, Calgary, Edmonton, Winnipeg, and Victoria, plus smaller university-affiliated sites — see our full Canada exam centers guide.

Session availability varies a lot by city — popular centers like Montréal and Toronto can fill within 24–48 hours of a new session opening, so book earlier than feels necessary.

How to prepare for TCF Canada

The exam rewards familiarity with its specific format as much as raw French ability — candidates who prepare with real timed conditions consistently outperform those who only study grammar and vocabulary in isolation. A few principles that make the biggest difference:

Practice under the real timer for each section, not just untimed review — 92 seconds per reading question feels very different once the clock is actually running.

Treat all four skills as equally urgent from day one. Most candidates' final file is held back by whichever single skill they neglected, not by their overall level.

Get every writing and speaking attempt corrected against the real CLB criteria, not just checked for grammar — examiners grade task achievement and coherence as much as accuracy.

Drill your actual recurring mistakes, not generic grammar drills — the errors you repeat are the ones worth spaced-repetition practice.

TCF Canada and your CRS score

For Express Entry candidates, the practical reason to care about all this is almost always Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) points. A strong TCF Canada result — CLB 7 or higher across all four skills — unlocks a meaningful French-language CRS bonus, and if you're also strong in English, a further bilingual bonus on top of that. It also opens eligibility for French-language category-based draws, which have run with cutoffs noticeably lower than general Express Entry draws in recent rounds. In practical terms, going from CLB 6 to CLB 7 is usually worth far more to your file than pushing an already-strong CLB 9 up to CLB 10 — if your study time is limited, that's where to point it. Frequently asked questions

How long does the TCF Canada exam take?

About 2 hours 47 minutes of total seat time across all four sections, plus check-in — plan for a half-day appointment.

Is there a pass mark for TCF Canada?

No. There's no pass or fail — you receive a CLB level per skill, and whether that's "enough" depends entirely on the minimum required by the specific program you're applying under.

How long is a TCF Canada result valid?

Two years from your test date. After that, most programs require a fresh result.

Can I retake only one section of TCF Canada?

No — TCF Canada is sat as a complete set of four sections each time. If you want to improve a single skill's score, you need to retake the full exam.

What's the difference between TCF Canada and TEF Canada?

Both test the same four skills, are accepted equally by IRCC, and map to the same CLB scale. The practical differences are in format and where each is offered — see our full comparison for details.

Is TCF Canada harder than TCF Québec or general TCF?

TCF Canada isn't harder or easier in terms of content difficulty — it's a distinct version built specifically for immigration purposes, using IRCC's conversion table. A TCF Québec or general TCF result is not automatically interchangeable with a TCF Canada result for federal immigration purposes.

Do I need to prepare separately for each of the four skills?

Yes, in the sense that each skill is scored and converted to CLB independently — a strong listening score cannot compensate for a weak writing score. Preparing all four, not just your strongest one or two, is the single biggest factor in a balanced result.

How much does TCF Canada cost?

Roughly $280–$320 CAD in Canada, or ₹25,000–₹30,000 in India, though exact pricing varies by center and changes over time — always confirm the current fee directly on your chosen center's registration page before paying.

Can I take TCF Canada online, from home?

No. TCF Canada must be taken in person at an authorized Alliance Française center — there is currently no remote or at-home version accepted for Canadian immigration purposes.

How soon can I retake TCF Canada if I'm not happy with my result?

This varies by center, but a minimum gap of around 20 days between attempts is common — check your specific center's policy, since some enforce longer waiting periods.

What should I bring on exam day?

A valid passport (or the specific ID your center requires), your booking confirmation printout, and nothing electronic — phones and smartwatches must be switched off and stored for the full duration.
