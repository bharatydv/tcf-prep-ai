Both are accepted equally by IRCC. Here's exactly how the formats differ, and how to pick the right one for you.

Quick answer: TCF Canada and TEF Canada are both accepted equally by IRCC for Express Entry, citizenship, and PNP applications, and both map to the identical CLB scale. Neither is officially easier — TCF uses a more compact scoring range and progressive-difficulty questions, while TEF has wider point scales per skill and is available at more test centers worldwide. Pick based on which is offered near you and which format suits how you test.

The short version

If you only need one fact: your CRS points, your CLB level, and your eligibility for any IRCC program are identical whether you take TCF Canada or TEF Canada, provided you hit the same benchmark on each. There is no immigration-outcome advantage to either test. The decision comes down to logistics and personal fit, not which one "counts for more."

By the numbers

  • Both tests convert to the same 12-level CLB/NCLC scale used across every IRCC program.
  • TCF Canada: 4 sections, ~2h 47min total seat time, scored out of 699 (listening/reading) and 20 (writing/speaking).
  • TEF Canada: 4 sections, ~2h total seat time, scored out of 360 (listening), 300 (reading), and 450 (writing/speaking).
  • TEF Canada's listening format changed as of September 1, 2025 — micro-trottoir questions now offer 3 options instead of 4, with some segments played twice.
  • Both results are valid for 2 years from the test date.

Where the formats actually differ

Writing: 3 shorter tasks vs. 2 longer ones

TCF Canada splits writing into three tâches sharing one 60-minute timer: a short practical message (60–120 words), a descriptive/narrative piece (120–150 words), and an argumentative essay (120–180 words). TEF Canada instead uses two tasks: Section A asks you to continue a news article (minimum 80 words, 25 minutes) and Section B asks you to argue a point of view (minimum 200 words, 35 minutes). If you write faster in short bursts, TCF's three-task structure may suit you better; if you prefer fewer, longer pieces where you can build an argument at length, TEF's structure may feel more natural.

Speaking: who asks the questions changes

This is the single biggest format difference between the two tests. TCF Canada's speaking section has three tâches: a guided self-introduction, an interactive roleplay where you ask an examiner questions to get information, and an opinion monologue. TEF Canada's speaking section has just two parts, but Section A specifically requires you to ask the examiner 10–12 questions to gather information (asking fewer than 8 noticeably hurts your score), and Section B has you defend a position while the examiner pushes back — candidates who try to deliver an uninterrupted monologue in Section B lose marks for failing to interact. TEF's speaking format catches more candidates off guard specifically because so few people practice "asking good questions under pressure" as a skill.

Listening and reading: question style

TCF Canada's comprehension papers use a progressive-difficulty format — early questions are worth fewer points, later ones more, so the back third of each section matters disproportionately for your final score. TEF Canada's listening section (updated as of September 2025) uses shorter micro-trottoir clips with 3 answer options instead of 4, with some interview segments played twice. Both are genuinely testing the same underlying comprehension ability; the difference is mostly in pacing rhythm and how partial credit is distributed.

Which one is actually easier?

Neither, in any measurable sense — both are accepted at face value by IRCC and both are designed to the same CEFR-aligned difficulty standards. What differs is which format plays to your personal strengths:

  • Choose TCF Canada if: you prefer more, shorter writing tasks over fewer long ones, and you're comfortable with a compact 0–20 scoring scale where small point differences matter more.
  • Choose TEF Canada if: you're confident asking structured questions out loud (Section A speaking), you prefer defending one sustained argument over three shorter written pieces, and a wider point scale (0–450) feels more forgiving to you.
  • Choose whichever is available near you if: this is honestly the deciding factor for most candidates. TCF Canada currently runs at a narrower set of test centers in some regions (for example, just four confirmed cities in India), while TEF Canada is offered more widely through the Alliance Française network and Chamber of Commerce partner centers.

Cost comparison

Pricing for both tests is broadly comparable and varies by country and center, generally landing in a similar range once converted to local currency — neither test is meaningfully cheaper than the other on average. Confirm current fees directly with your nearest center, since both France Éducation International (TCF) and the Paris Chamber of Commerce (TEF, via Le français des affaires) set and revise pricing independently by region.

Which programs accept which test?

Both TCF Canada and TEF Canada are accepted, with no preference either way, across Express Entry (including French-language category-based draws), Canadian citizenship applications, and the great majority of Provincial Nominee Program streams that set a French-language requirement. Neither test is designated as the "primary" or "backup" option — IRCC's own guidance treats a valid result from either as interchangeable proof of French proficiency at the same CLB level. The one place this can differ is a specific PNP stream or employer-specific requirement that names one test explicitly, which is uncommon but worth checking on the program's own page before you book.

Can I switch between them if I don't like my first result?

Yes — there's no rule preventing you from taking TCF Canada for one attempt and TEF Canada for the next. Some candidates deliberately do this after a result that fell short in one skill, on the theory that a different format might suit them better for that specific skill. There's no penalty for mixing tests across attempts, as long as your most recent valid result for each application meets the program's requirement.

Frequently asked questions

Is TCF Canada or TEF Canada easier?

Neither is officially easier — both are accepted equally by IRCC and calibrated to the same CEFR standards. The right choice depends on which format suits your personal test-taking style and which is available near you.

Do TCF Canada and TEF Canada give the same CRS points?

Yes. Both convert to the identical CLB/NCLC scale, and CRS points are calculated from your CLB level, not from which test you took to get there.

Which test is more widely available?

TEF Canada generally runs at more test centers globally through the Alliance Française and Chamber of Commerce network. TCF Canada's availability varies more by region — check our exam centres guides for India and Canada for confirmed locations.

Can I take TCF Canada and TEF Canada and submit whichever result is better?

You can take either or both, but check your specific program's requirements — most ask for one valid, current result per application rather than letting you mix scores from two different tests within the same submission.

Is the speaking section really that different between the two?

Yes — this is the biggest practical difference. TEF Canada's Section A specifically requires you to ask the examiner questions (a skill most candidates don't practice), while TCF Canada's speaking tâches are more evenly split between guided introduction, roleplay, and monologue.

Does switching from TCF to TEF (or vice versa) mean starting my preparation over?

No — the underlying French skills you need are identical. What changes is your familiarity with each format's specific task structure and timing, which typically takes a few practice sessions to adjust to, not a full restart.
